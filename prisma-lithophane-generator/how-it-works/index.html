How It Works

Aug 07, 20265 min read

Introduction

This section of documentation explains what the system does internally and why. None of what is described here is required to successfully make a lithophane. My goal is to help you develop a rough mental model of how Prisma Generator works. This will hopefully allow you to Prisma Generator in an informed and intentional way to produce lithophanes that look how you want them to.

Believe your lying eyes

When you perceive an image on a screen or on a printed page, the wide range of colors you see are the result of a kind of visual magic trick. By using visual building blocks that are too small to individually see, a small set of primary colors can create the appearance of many, many colors. Monitors use the red, green, and blue (RGB) as “additive primaries”. Most printers use cyan, magenta, yellow, and black (CMYK) as “subtractive primaries”.footnote Neither approach cannot reproduce the entire range of colors found in the real world, but perfect color fidelity is not required for an image to look “good enough” to your eye. There is no single “correct” method for producing an image from a small set of primary color components. The correct approach depends on what your goals are and what image you are trying to reproduce.

3D printers are not normal printers

Like printers, color lithophane generation tools also use CMYK to produce their colors. Because black filament tends to be quite opaque, they use white filament instead of black as the key color component (the K in CMYK). There is nothing intrinsically wrong with this approach. It works well at reproducing rich, saturated colors like those commonly found in logos, illustrations, and other types of graphic art. It utterly fails at reproducing the wide range of subtle colors commonly found in real photographs due to the different constraints of printing on paper vs printing with layers of plastic.

In typical color printing, the range of colors is achieved by changing the size and density of the colored dots (“half-toning”) that are patterned across the printed image. Color lithophanes do not have this luxury. They are more like a stack of layers of stained glass. Every part of the image must be filled with plastic. Layers can only be so thin. The smallest features have to be roughly the size of the nozzle. This limits the range of colors that can be made and how fine the finest details can be.

The complicated nature of the Prisma Generator’s pipeline and its many settings is an attempt to answer the question: “How can I reproduce any image in a color lithophane as accurately as possible given the many limitations of 3D printers?”

The pipeline

The pipeline used by Prisma Generator is my answer to that question. Instead of using CMYK, the filaments used are selected based on the colors actually found in an image. Then that image is processed in a series of stages to convert its pixels into regions of color that are actually printable.

The diagram below depicts the stages that occur to convert an image into a group of 3D meshes, one for each filament color.

The main flow runs left to right; the palette’s color model and the lookup table feed the solve from below. Select any stage to read how it works.

The stages

  • Image Adjustments — the framing and color grading you apply to the source photo in the first tab, before the automatic pipeline begins.
  • Preprocessing — an optional, configurable clean-up pass that conditions the image toward what the chosen palette can actually reproduce.
  • Target Colors — translating every pixel into a perceptual color space and onto the set of colors the palette can physically reach.
  • The Solve — the core step: choosing a filament recipe for each region of the image so the backlit result matches the targets while staying printable.
  • White Cap — converting the solved colors into a thickness map and adding a white layer that sets overall brightness.
  • Runs with Swaps — planning flat filament bands when the palette contains more colors than the printer can hold at once.
  • Export — turning the finished layers into an STL or 3MF model the slicer can open.

Feeding the solve from the side:

  • Palette — the filaments chosen for a print, and how Prisma can suggest them from the image.
  • Color Model — the model that predicts how stacks of filaments will appear to your eye when backlit.
  • The Color LUT — a precomputed table of every printable stack and the color it produces that the solver uses instead of calcuating them from scratch every time.

Settings as a cross-cutting view

The pipeline pages linked above describe this process as it occurs from stage to stage. Settings: How They Work describes this same process, but organized around the specific options found in the Settings drawer sections— Essentials, Pre-processing, Color Solver, and White Cap — and explains how their controls change those stages internally.
